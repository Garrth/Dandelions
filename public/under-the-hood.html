Dandelions Join

Under the hood

Dandelions is a way to fund the work people and the planet need, by creating points only when that work is verified done. This is the short version of how it works, about a 10 minute read. The goal it serves is set in the charter, and the measured targets are on the Baselines page.

1. The problem: plenty to do, and the work waits

AI and automation are going to change a lot of jobs, and nobody has a real plan for what comes next. At the same time, there is more useful work waiting than people to do it: clean energy, a better grid, healthy soil, clean water, care for kids and elders, housing, training.

The people are here and the materials are here. What's missing is a way to put them together.

Why the work waits. Most dollars today start out as loans, and a loan has to be paid back with interest. So the work that gets funded first is the work that pays back fast. Roads, clean water, healthy soil and caring for people pay back slowly, or in ways nobody can send a bill for. So they wait, no matter how many people are ready to do them.

The market is great at a lot of things, but it isn't built for this. Government runs on taxes and borrowing, which run short right when jobs disappear. And waiting for billionaires or charity to step in isn't a plan.

Andy, picture a crew next to a pile of lumber, ready to go. And the boss says, "Sorry folks, we're out of inches."

The idea in one line: human and natural resources are the real limit, not dollars. Dandelions tests a system where the work itself is what brings the points into being.

2. The idea: points that come from work

Dandelions points are created only when verified work gets done. They are not lent into existence and carry no debt or interest. No work, no points.

  • Debt-free. Nobody has to pay points back, so a project doesn't need to earn a profit to be worth doing. It needs to serve the mission.
  • Work first. Points are created in small pieces as each milestone of a project is checked and confirmed.
  • For spending, not hoarding. Points are meant to move: people earn them, then spend them at local businesses that accept them.
  • A parallel layer. Dandelions doesn't replace the market. It aims at the work the market won't fund: infrastructure, healthcare, care work, and restoring the planet.

Hold on, Dandy. So the points only show up when the work does?

That's the whole trick, Andy. Nobody gets to make them just by deciding to.

Where things stand today: points aren't turned on yet and have no cash value. Right now Dandelions is pledges, a business directory, Talk, and Rai. Points come later, one tested step at a time.

Why businesses matter. A point is only useful if you can spend it. So signing up local businesses that will accept points is one of the first and most important jobs. How many points a county can create each month is tied to how many points its businesses are ready to accept.

3. How a county grows

Dandelions grows county by county, and each county unlocks more as more of its people take part. Progress is measured as the share of the county's population that has pledged.

StageWhenWhat opens
SeedThe first pledge in the countyThe county shows on the map. The directory, Talk and Rai are open to everyone.
Sprout1% of the county has pledgedPhase 2: small local projects people can choose to join, starting with signing up businesses and helping them accept points.
Bloom5% have pledgedBigger local projects, more roles, more checkers.
Puffball10% have pledgedThe county is ready for its biggest projects. It also helps nearby counties grow.

There's no set timeline. A county grows as fast as its people take part. The details of each stage are published before a county reaches it.

4. How work gets picked, done and checked

Every project follows the same public steps, from the needs list to points paid for each checked milestone.

  1. The needs list. Real data ranks what each area needs most to meet the charter's goals and the baselines. Rai helps build the list and shows its reasoning and data in public.
  2. A project is proposed. An approved project owner picks work from the list and proposes it: what gets done, the milestones, and the price in points. Project owners are ID-checked, and so is their business.
  3. The proposal is checked. A paid checker reviews every proposal before it starts: is the work real, needed, and priced fairly?
  4. Sized to the owner's record. A new owner starts small, at about 500 points a project. After that, a project can be up to twice the size of the biggest one they've finished.
  5. The work gets done. Members join the project and do the work.
  6. Each milestone is checked. Several checkers, picked at random and paid for each check, confirm the milestone is really done. Faking work doesn't get past people who don't know each other and are each paid to look.
  7. Points are created and paid. When a milestone passes, the code creates the points for it and pays them out. Nobody creates points by deciding to.

A project that stalls is cancelled, and that goes on the owner's record. Projects can't be split into sub-projects to get around the size limit.

5. What keeps it honest

The rules are built so points stay tied to real work, real people and real things to buy.

  • Capped by what's really there. Approvals can't go past the workers and materials available in an area. When labor is short, projects wait in line, open projects finish first, shortages are posted publicly, and paid training fills the gap.
  • Capped by what businesses accept. Each month's payouts are limited by how many points local businesses and landlords have said they'll accept. That keeps points spendable.
  • Make way for the market. When businesses need workers, new projects slow down. When private hiring drops, projects pick up the slack.
  • Keep it moving. Points are for spending. Idle points go back into circulation or into real things, like infrastructure, that hold value.
  • One person, one account. ID checks make sure every member, project owner and vote is one real person. Dandelions only keeps a pass or fail from the check, never your ID papers.
  • Everything in the open. Rules, data, point flows and Rai's reasoning are public and checkable. The code is open source.

6. Who decides

No one is in charge. The goal, the open rules and public data keep everyone pulling the same way. We call it decentralized unity: many people and groups, each running their own work, all pulling toward one shared goal.

WhoWhat they doWhat they never do
The charterSets the goal everything servesChanges without a community vote
Rai (AI)Ranks needs from real data, sizes projects, estimates local capacity, flags possible fraud, and shows its reasoning publiclyCreates points, approves projects or changes rules
PeoplePropose projects, do the work, check milestones, settle disputes, and vote. They can overrule Rai, with a high bar like a supermajorityCreate points by deciding to
CodeCreates and pays points automatically when checked work meets the rulesMakes exceptions

Changing the rules. Members propose changes to the charter and the baselines, talk them over in Talk, and vote. One person, one vote, and only ID-checked members vote. Votes are private, and results show when the vote closes.

7. The road ahead

Dandelions moves control from its starting team to its members in steps, each one in the open.

  1. Open code. Today the site runs on open-source code anyone can read and check.
  2. Public data. The data behind decisions, like the needs list and point totals, becomes public and checkable.
  3. A public record of points. Points start in Dandelions' own records, with a daily fingerprint published publicly so nobody can quietly change the past. Later the record moves to a shared public ledger run by many independent operators.
  4. Run by its members. The community takes over the rules by vote, and the jobs that run Dandelions (checking, keeping the record, reviewing code, site security) become paid roles anyone qualified can take.

Version 1 of this plan came from one person. It's a starting point, and it's meant to change as the people who take part improve it.

8. What we don't know yet

The biggest question is whether it will work. We don't know. The only way to find out is to try it, and the only way to try it is for enough people to take part.

  • The first projects. They'll depend on what each county needs, and they'll take shape once a county reaches Sprout.
  • The exact numbers. How fast points are created, project size limits, how many checkers each milestone needs. These are starting points, and members can change them by vote.
  • How fast it grows. There's no timeline. It grows as fast as people take part.

We'll be honest about how it's going, including what doesn't work.
